Undyed: what changes when you take the dye house out of the supply chain

By Priya Raghunathan, fabric sourcing and compliance Published May 21, 2026 Updated June 4, 2026 About 8 minutes

Twenty-four of our thirty-one pieces have never been near a dye. That decision changed more about how this company runs than any other single choice, and not all of it in the direction you would guess.

What a dye house actually is

Dyeing textiles at scale is a wet chemical process and a fairly heavy one. Cloth or yarn is loaded into a jet, a jig or a beam machine, submerged in a dye liquor, heated, held, then rinsed repeatedly until the water runs clear. For reactive dyeing on cellulose, which is what linen and cotton normally get, the bath needs salt, usually a lot of it, plus alkali to fix the dye, plus a soaping stage afterwards to remove the portion that never bonded.

That unfixed portion is the part people underestimate. Reactive dye fixation rates commonly sit somewhere between sixty and eighty per cent, which means a meaningful fraction of the dye you paid for goes down the drain and has to be treated before it goes anywhere else. A well-run mill with a modern effluent plant handles this properly. A badly run one does not, and the coloured rivers you occasionally see photographed near textile towns are the visible end of that.

The energy side matters as much as the water. The bath has to be brought up to temperature and held there, then the cloth has to be dried. Textile Exchange's 2024 materials report puts dyeing and finishing at roughly a fifth of a typical garment's cradle-to-gate energy. That is a published estimate, not our measurement, and I want to be clear about which is which.

What we removed, and what we did not

We do not dye 24 of our 31 pieces. The linen arrives from Lithuania scoured and washed but uncoloured. The wool arrives scoured, sorted by natural fleece colour, and undyed. The organic cotton is knitted and woven in North Carolina without colour.

Seven pieces do get colour. The Field Stripe Shirt is yarn dyed with a low-impact indigo before weaving. The Sage Washed Linen Shirt and the olive colourway of the Woven Linen Tote are plant dyed with weld and iron in small runs. Those are the exceptions, they are labelled as such on every product page, and they are the pieces with the shortest colour stability.

What we did not remove is scouring. Raw flax and raw wool both need washing, and wool scouring in particular is a real wet process with real effluent. Anybody claiming a completely dry supply chain for a natural fibre is either using a fibre that does not need scouring or is not counting the mill's water.

Four things that changed

Lead time collapsed

This was the surprise. Dyeing is usually a separate facility with its own queue, and small orders wait behind large ones. When we dyed, a fabric order took eleven to fourteen weeks from purchase order to delivery at our door. Undyed, it takes six to eight. That single change is why we can run production every six weeks instead of every quarter, and it is the operational reason our stock outages last two weeks rather than two months.

Minimum order quantities dropped

A dye lot has a floor. Below a certain yardage the mill cannot economically fill the machine, and you end up buying more cloth than you need or paying a surcharge that makes the garment unviable. Undyed cloth is woven to order from stock yarn, so our minimums fell from about 1,500 yards per colour to about 400 yards. That is the difference between a range we can actually hold and a range we would have to gamble on.

Colour consistency got worse

This is the cost, and it is a real one. A dye house exists partly to make everything match. Its entire quality system is built around hitting a colour standard within a tolerance, batch after batch. Take it away and you are left with whatever the fibre is.

Flax colour varies with the growing season, the retting conditions and the field. Wool varies with the animal. Our oat linen has moved through a range of perhaps a tone and a half over seven years, and two of our shirts bought eighteen months apart will not match if you hold them against each other in daylight. We now say this on every product page rather than hoping nobody notices.

A dye house is a consistency machine. Removing it does not just remove colour; it removes the apparatus that was making everything look the same.

Customers started talking about the cloth differently

Before, the emails we got were about shade: is this more grey or more beige, will it match the trousers. Now they are about texture, weight and slub. That is partly because colour was doing a lot of the descriptive work and, once it is gone, people look at the other properties. It has made the product pages better, because we had to learn to describe cloth without leaning on a colour name.

The three complaints this reliably generates

It is not white enough. Undyed linen is a warm grey-beige. Consumers are used to optical brightening agents, fluorescent compounds added to detergent and to fabric finishing that absorb ultraviolet light and re-emit it as blue, making cloth read whiter than white. Our cloth has none. Next to a supermarket white shirt it looks dingy. Next to natural daylight and nothing else, it looks like cloth.

It does not match my other one. Covered above. We now recommend buying the pieces you want to wear together in a single order, because a single order almost always comes from a single fabric batch.

It changes over time. Undyed cellulose lightens gently in sunlight, and undyed wool warms very slightly. This is even, not patchy, because there is no dye to lift unevenly. Some people love this and some people find it unsettling. Both reactions are reasonable and we would rather set the expectation than argue about it later.

What we cannot claim

We cannot tell you how much water this saves per garment, because we do not have metered data from our mills and I am not going to reverse-engineer a number from an industry average and present it as ours. We made that mistake once, a customer with an environmental engineering background caught it, and we took the figure down. The sustainability page now separates what we measure from what we estimate and names the source for every estimate.

What I can say with confidence is structural rather than numerical. Dyeing is a wet, hot, chemical process. Removing it from 24 of 31 products removes that process for those products. The direction of the effect is not in doubt; the magnitude is, and I would rather say so.

Would we do it again

Yes, and mostly for the operational reasons rather than the environmental ones. Shorter lead times and lower minimums are what let a company this size hold a permanent range instead of chasing seasons. The environmental benefit is real but it is the one I can quantify least well, which is an uncomfortable thing for a sustainability-adjacent brand to admit and is also true.

The thing we underestimated was how much explaining it would require. Undyed is not a colour story you can put on a swing tag. It is a set of behaviours the customer has to understand before they buy, or they will be disappointed after. This piece exists because we would rather do the explaining up front.
