Thirty pieces, ten years: building a wardrobe that does not reset every season

By Nell Fitzsimmons, production manager Published March 18, 2026 About 10 minutes

Most capsule wardrobe advice fails for the same reason most diets fail: it describes a destination and skips the mechanism. Here is the mechanism, including the parts that are inconvenient for a company that sells clothes.

Why the usual advice does not work

The standard capsule wardrobe article gives you a number, usually between thirty and forty, and a list. Two white shirts, one blazer, three knits, and so on. The number is arbitrary and the list is somebody else's life. If your job requires steel toe caps or a courtroom, the list is useless. If you live somewhere with a genuine winter, the list is dangerous.

The deeper problem is that a list describes a static end state, and a wardrobe is not static. Things wear out, your body changes, you move house, you change job. A method that only works if nothing changes is not a method.

What actually holds up is a rule about how new things enter and old things leave, applied consistently for years. That is boring, and it is why it works.

Start by counting what you wear, not what you own

For four weeks, hang everything you wear back on the left-hand side of the rail after it is washed. Do not reorganise, do not tidy, do not make decisions. At the end of four weeks look at where the divide falls.

Almost everybody finds the same shape: a small cluster on the left that they wear constantly, and a long tail on the right that they have not touched. When we did this internally across eleven people, the median was 19 items worn in four weeks out of a median 68 items owned. One person wore 12 things out of 94.

That left-hand cluster is your actual wardrobe. Everything else is inventory. The point of the exercise is not to throw the inventory away, at least not yet. It is to know the number, because you cannot reason about a wardrobe you have never counted.

The four questions

Once you know your cluster, every potential purchase gets four questions. They are deliberately harsh and they are why our own staff buy less from us than you might expect.

One: does it go with at least five things I already wear? Not five things I own. Five things from the left-hand cluster. A garment that only works with one other garment is a two-item purchase pretending to be a one-item purchase.

Two: will I wear it in at least two of the four seasons? A pure summer piece has to work about four times as hard per dollar to justify itself. This is the question that rules out most of what the industry sells, and it is the reason our range has no obviously seasonal pieces in it.

Three: can I care for it the way I actually live? If it needs dry cleaning and you have never once been to a dry cleaner, you are buying a garment you will wear four times. Be honest about the version of you that exists on a Tuesday evening, not the aspirational one.

Four: what is it replacing? If the answer is nothing, the wardrobe grows. That is allowed occasionally and it should be a conscious decision rather than a default.

The cost per wear arithmetic, done properly

Cost per wear is usually deployed as a rhetorical device to justify an expensive purchase. Done honestly it is more interesting, because it usually tells you to buy the mid-priced thing.

Take a shirt at $145 that you wear once a week for six years. That is roughly 300 wears, so about 48 cents a wear. Now take a $35 shirt worn once a week that lasts eighteen months before the collar frays: 78 wears, about 45 cents a wear. The cheap shirt wins, narrowly, on this measure alone.

What the simple calculation misses is everything around the garment. The $35 shirt has to be replaced four times over the same period, which is four shopping decisions, four deliveries, four rounds of finding out the fit changed. And the second, third and fourth shirt are rarely the same shirt, because cheap ranges turn over constantly.

The real argument for a durable garment is not that it is cheaper per wear. Often it is roughly the same. It is that it removes the repeated decision. If you know what shirt you wear, you stop shopping for shirts, and the time and attention that frees up is worth considerably more than three cents a wear.

That is also the honest limit of the argument. If money is tight now, a $145 shirt is not a clever investment, it is a $145 shirt. Buy the affordable thing and take good care of it. Care does more for lifespan than price does.

Care is most of durability

We see returns and repairs, so we get an unusual view of how garments actually fail. The overwhelming majority of premature failures we see are care failures, not manufacturing ones.

  • Wool felted in a hot wash. Not reversible, and it is the single most common one.
  • Linen and cotton worn thin at the fold from tumble drying. Heat plus agitation abrades fibre.
  • Knitwear with a dropped shoulder from being hung wet on a narrow hanger.
  • Oil stains set by washing before treating, which fixes them permanently.
  • Buttons lost because the thread wore through and nobody noticed until it was gone.

None of those are expensive to prevent. Wash less often and cooler, hang things damp, dry knits flat, treat stains before washing, and check buttons twice a year. That list is worth more to the lifespan of your clothes than any purchasing decision you will make.

What thirty pieces actually looks like

For a temperate US climate and an office that has no formal dress code, a workable shape is roughly: eight tops you wear constantly, four bottoms, three knits, two jackets, one coat, two pairs of shoes in regular rotation, and a tail of eight to ten things that are occasion-specific or seasonal. That is about thirty items in active use.

The specific numbers matter far less than the ratio. Most people are top-heavy, owning fifteen shirts and two pairs of trousers, and then complaining they have nothing to wear. You have nothing to wear because the constraint is at the bottom, not the top. Count your cluster and see where it is thin.

The uncomfortable part

A company that sells clothes has an obvious interest in you buying more of them. Everything in this article points the other way, and I want to be straightforward about how that is commercially survivable for us rather than pretend we are being selfless.

It works because our margin comes from selling directly rather than from volume. A wholesale brand needs to sell roughly twice as many units to make the same money, which is why the industry is structured around newness. We can be a company that eleven thousand people buy two things from every couple of years. We could not be a company that four hundred people buy forty things from, and we are not trying to be.

It also works because a customer who buys carefully returns less. Our return rate is 9.2 per cent, against a US apparel average that is commonly reported somewhere between 20 and 30 per cent. Returns are expensive. Every return we do not process pays for a good deal of the explaining we do on these pages.

Where to start, concretely

  • Do the four-week rail exercise before you buy anything. It costs nothing and it is the only part of this that is not opinion.
  • Fix the thinnest part of your cluster first, which is usually bottoms or outerwear.
  • Apply the four questions to the next thing you are tempted by. Most things fail question one.
  • Improve your care routine before you improve your purchasing. It is cheaper and it works faster.
  • Buy things that stay in production, so a replacement is a reorder rather than a search.

That last point is the one we can actually help with. Our range has not been reset since 2019, and the Weaver Band-Collar Shirt you buy this year is the same pattern as the one you bought in 2022, four small revisions later.
