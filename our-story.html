Since 2019, Asheville, North Carolina

One shirt pattern and a roll of Lithuanian linen.

That was the whole business in March 2019. Eleven people work here now and the shirt is on its fourth pattern revision.

  1. Home
  2. Our story

Where it came from

Marguerite Okonkwo spent nine years as a technical designer for two large American apparel companies, mostly working on the part of the job nobody writes about: turning a designer's sketch into a specification a factory can actually sew, then negotiating that specification down until it hits a target cost. She got good at it. She also watched a shirt she had spent four months perfecting get value-engineered from a 6 oz linen to a 45% polyester blend three weeks before production, because the price point had moved.

She left in late 2018 and spent the winter making shirts on a domestic machine in a rented room on Alder Mill Road. The first roll of linen came from a Lithuanian mill she had used at her old job and could still call. She made forty-one shirts, sold thirty-eight to people she knew, and used the money to buy a used industrial straight stitch.

The idea was not complicated: hold one specification, never change it to hit a price, and charge whatever that specification actually costs. Every decision since has been a version of that.

How the business is put together

There is no clever structure here. It is worth writing down anyway, because most clothing brands do not, and the structure is what determines what a garment can be.

Direct only, with eleven exceptions

Ninety-four per cent of what we sell goes through this website. The rest goes to eleven independent shops that buy at wholesale. We do not sell on marketplaces, we do not drop-ship, and we have turned down every department store that has asked, because a department store needs a markdown calendar and we do not have one.

One price, held

A piece costs what it costs. We do not run seasonal sales, we do not do Black Friday, and we have raised prices exactly twice, both times because fabric costs moved and both times announced in advance by email. If you buy in March and the price drops in October, that has never happened and is not part of the plan.

Small batches, not made to stock

We cut in runs of 60 to 200 units depending on the piece, roughly every six weeks. That is inefficient per unit and it is the reason we occasionally sell out of a size for two weeks. It also means we are never sitting on a warehouse of unsold stock that has to be discounted or destroyed.

Eleven people, one building

Six in the cutting and sewing room, two in customer service and shipping, one pattern cutter, one who handles fabric sourcing and compliance, and Marguerite. Everyone is a direct employee. Nobody here is paid per piece.

What a $145 shirt is made of, financially

We get asked this often enough that it is worth answering plainly. These are averages across a production run of the Weaver Band-Collar Shirt, rounded to the nearest dollar.

Cost breakdown for one Weaver Band-Collar Shirt at a retail price of $145 USD.
LineAmountNote
Fabric$312.4 yards of 5.5 oz Lithuanian linen, including cutting waste
Trim$4Corozo buttons, cotton thread, woven label, care label
Labour$38Cutting, sewing, pressing and inspection, at our wage floor
Overhead$22Rent, machines, utilities, insurance, software
Shipping and packaging$9Averaged across free and flat-rate orders
Payment and returns$13Card fees plus the cost of a roughly 9% return rate
Margin$28What is left. It pays for growth, and some years for very little

A comparable shirt from a brand selling through wholesale would need to cost about $290 at retail to leave the same margin, because the wholesale price has to be roughly half the retail price. That gap is the entire argument for selling direct.

Things we have got wrong

In 2021 we launched a linen-viscose blend because a mill agent convinced us it would solve the wrinkling complaints. It did, and it also pilled at the underarm within four months and failed at the side seam on three garments in the first year. We pulled it, refunded everyone who had bought one whether or not they had complained, and it cost us about eleven thousand dollars. It was the right call and we should have made it a year earlier.

In 2022 we tried a subscription. The idea was two pieces a year at a lower combined price. It worked commercially and it was wrong: it pushed people to buy on a schedule rather than when they needed something, which is the exact behaviour we say we are against. We closed it after eight months and honoured every commitment.

In 2023 we published a carbon figure per garment that we could not properly substantiate. A customer with an environmental engineering background wrote to ask how we had calculated it, and the honest answer was that we had used an industry average and presented it as our own measurement. We removed it. Everything on the sustainability page now is either measured here or clearly labelled as an estimate with its source.

What we will not do

  • Run a countdown timer, a stock counter or any other manufactured urgency.
  • Publish a review we did not receive, or edit one we did.
  • Use the word sustainable without a number or a certificate behind it.
  • Sell your email address, your order history or your browsing behaviour.
  • Change a specification to hit a price without changing the price.
