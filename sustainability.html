What we measure, what we estimate, and what we do not know.

Clothing companies make a lot of environmental claims that cannot be checked. This page separates the three categories: figures measured at our own facility, figures estimated from a named published source, and the parts of the chain where we genuinely do not have visibility.

If something here is wrong, we want to know. Email hello@slowlinenorganic.com and we will correct it publicly, as we did in 2023 when a customer showed us our carbon figure was borrowed rather than measured.

The four fibres, and where each one comes from

European flax linen

Grown in Normandy, France and in Flanders, Belgium. Flax for linen needs no irrigation in that climate and, in a normal year, no pesticide beyond a pre-emergence herbicide. It is dew-retted in the field rather than water-retted in tanks, which removes the single most polluting step in traditional linen processing.

  • Spun: wet spun in Lithuania for a smoother, stronger yarn
  • Woven: Panevėžys, Lithuania
  • Certification: European Flax, OEKO-TEX Standard 100 Class II
  • Used in: 19 of 31 pieces

GOTS organic cotton

Grown in the Texas High Plains and in Georgia, ginned in Lubbock, knitted and woven in North Carolina. GOTS certification covers the whole chain, not just the farm, which is the part most cotton claims quietly omit.

  • Grown: Texas and Georgia, rain-fed where possible
  • Knitted: Gastonia, North Carolina
  • Certification: GOTS 6.0, scope certificate available on request
  • Used in: 7 of 31 pieces

Hemp, blended with linen

Grown in France and processed alongside the flax. Hemp gives us tensile strength that flax alone cannot, which is why every jacket in the range uses it. It needs no irrigation and it suppresses weeds well enough to need very little herbicide.

  • Grown: Champagne region, France
  • Woven: Lithuania, on the same looms as the flax
  • Certification: OEKO-TEX Standard 100 Class II
  • Used in: 3 of 31 pieces

Undyed lambswool and merino

Sourced through a wool pool in Wyoming and Montana and scoured in South Carolina. Every supplier certifies non-mulesed. We buy the natural fleece colours rather than dyeing, which removes an entire wet processing stage.

  • Grown: Wyoming and Montana, US wool pool
  • Knitted: Rutherfordton, North Carolina
  • Certification: non-mulesed declaration, OEKO-TEX Standard 100
  • Used in: 5 of 31 pieces

Figures we estimate, with the source named

These are not our measurements. They come from published life-cycle assessments for the fibre type and are adjusted for our weights. Treat them as order-of-magnitude, not precision.

Linen versus cotton water use

Rain-fed European flax uses on the order of one twentieth the irrigation water of conventionally grown cotton. Source: European Confederation of Flax and Hemp published figures, 2023. We have not independently verified this.

Skipping the dye house

Dyeing and finishing is commonly cited as roughly a fifth of a garment's total energy footprint. Leaving undyed removes most of that for 24 of our 31 pieces. Source: Textile Exchange Materials Market Report, 2024.

Garment lifespan

We believe our pieces last longer than the industry average and we cannot prove it, because the label is seven years old and the honest test takes fifteen. What we can say is that our repair rate is low and our warranty claims are running under 0.4% of units sold.

What we do not know

Naming the gaps is more useful than filling them with averages.

Energy use at the mills

We know where our cloth is woven and we have visited both mills. We do not have their metered energy data and they are not obliged to give it to us. Any per-garment carbon figure we published would therefore be an assumption dressed as a measurement, which is why we do not publish one.

The farm level

Flax is bought through a Lithuanian spinner who buys from French and Belgian growers. We know the region. We do not know the individual farm for any given roll, and anyone who tells you they do for a commodity fibre at our volume is probably overstating it.

What happens after you

We have no take-back scheme, so we lose sight of a garment at the point of sale. Linen and wool are both compostable in principle. Whether yours ends up composted, resold or in landfill is not something we can currently influence or measure.

Shipping emissions

Fabric comes from Europe by sea and moves inland by truck. Parcels go out by USPS and UPS. We have not modelled this and we would rather say so than borrow a number from a calculator that does not know our routes.

Repair, resale and end of life

If a seam, a buttonhole or a knitted join fails within five years of purchase under normal wear, we repair it free or replace the garment if repair is not sensible. Send a photograph to hello@slowlinenorganic.com with your order number. This does not cover damage, moth holes, or a garment that has simply worn thin with age, which is what we want to happen.

We will repair anything we made, at any age, for the cost of the labour. Typical charges are $15 to reset a button and rework a buttonhole, $25 to patch an elbow, $40 to replace a pocket bag. Postage each way is yours. Email first with photographs and we will quote before you send.

Garments that fail inspection are not destroyed and are not sold as first quality. Staff take what they want, and the remainder goes to a clothing bank run by a shelter on Coxe Avenue in Asheville. We do not run a seconds sale because sorting and photographing them costs more than the garments are worth.

Orders ship in an unbleached kraft mailer with a paper tape seal, no plastic polybag and no tissue. The mailer is curbside recyclable across most of the US. The only plastic in the parcel is the shipping label face sheet, which we have not yet found a workable alternative to at our volume.
