Why linen wrinkles, and why we stopped apologising for it

By Marguerite Okonkwo, founder and pattern cutter Published July 2, 2026 About 9 minutes

Roughly one email in twenty that reaches our customer service inbox is about creasing. For four years we answered them apologetically. We do not any more, and this piece is the long version of why.

The crease starts inside the fibre, not in the weave

A flax fibre is a bundle of long cellulose chains held together by pectin and hemicellulose, arranged in a spiral around a hollow core called a lumen. The chains are highly crystalline, meaning they are packed in a regular, ordered structure over most of their length. Crystalline cellulose is stiff and strong. It is the reason a linen shirt can outlast a cotton one by a decade and the reason linen thread was used for sailcloth and bookbinding long before anyone thought of it as a summer fabric.

Stiffness and elasticity are opposites. A cotton fibre is convoluted, twisted like a ribbon, with far more amorphous, disordered regions between its crystalline zones. When you bend cotton those amorphous regions absorb the deformation and, given a little moisture and time, spring back. Wool goes further: its keratin protein contains coiled helices and disulphide cross-links that act like tiny springs, which is why a wool suit hung overnight in a steamy bathroom looks pressed in the morning.

Flax has almost none of that recovery mechanism. When you fold a linen fibre sharply, the hydrogen bonds between the cellulose chains break at the fold and re-form in the new position. The fibre does not remember its old shape because, at a molecular level, it now has a new one. That is a crease. It takes water or steam to break those bonds again and let the fibre lie flat.

So the wrinkle is not a symptom of loose weaving, a low thread count, poor finishing or a cheap mill. It is the direct consequence of the property that makes linen worth wearing at all. You cannot have the strength and the drape and the cool hand without it.

The two ways the industry suppresses it

There are essentially two commercial approaches, and both have a cost that is rarely stated on the swing tag.

Resin finishing

The dominant method is a cross-linking resin, historically a formaldehyde-based one and now usually a modified DMDHEU compound. The resin is padded onto the cloth and cured with heat, chemically bonding adjacent cellulose chains so they cannot slide past each other. The fabric becomes dimensionally stable and genuinely resists creasing. It also becomes measurably weaker.

Published textile chemistry puts the tensile strength loss from a full easy-care resin finish somewhere between twenty and forty per cent, with abrasion resistance falling similarly. In practice a resin-finished linen shirt has a shorter life than an unfinished one, and it fails in a specific way: the fabric goes thin and then splits at the collar fold and the elbow, rather than wearing evenly. The hand changes too. Resin-finished linen has a slightly papery, slick feel and it does not soften with washing, because the cross-links that stop it creasing also stop the fibres moving against one another.

Blending

The second approach is to blend flax with something more elastic. Linen-viscose, linen-polyester and linen-lyocell blends all crease less than pure linen, roughly in proportion to how much of the flax has been replaced. A 55/45 linen-viscose feels lovely on the roll and drapes beautifully.

We tried exactly that in 2021 and it is one of our more expensive mistakes. The blend solved the wrinkling complaints and introduced two new problems: it pilled at the underarm within four months, and on three garments in the first year it failed at the side seam, because viscose is weak when wet and our customers, correctly, wash their clothes. We pulled the fabric, refunded everyone who had bought one whether they had complained or not, and it cost us about eleven thousand dollars. The longer account is on the our story page.

A finish that stops linen creasing is a finish that stops linen behaving like linen. There is no version of this trade where you get the crease resistance for free.

What actually changes how much a linen garment creases

Within unfinished, pure linen there is still real variation, and it comes from four things.

Weight. A 4.5 oz open weave creases more visibly than an 11 oz canvas, because the lighter cloth has less mass holding it flat and the folds are sharper. This is why our jackets look tidier at the end of a day than our shirts do.

Yarn preparation. Wet-spun flax yarn is smoother and more even than dry-spun, which produces a cloth with fewer slubs and a more uniform crease pattern. All our linen is wet spun, which is more expensive and is the main reason our cloth costs about a third more per yard than a commodity linen.

Weave structure. A plain weave has the maximum number of interlacing points and therefore the maximum resistance to yarn movement, so it creases sharply. A twill or a leno has longer floats, and the yarns can shift slightly instead of folding. Our Open Weave Camp Shirt is a leno for exactly this reason and it visibly recovers better than the plain weave shirting.

Washing and drying. This is the one you control. Linen dried in a tumble dryer creases hard, because the fibres are being folded repeatedly while hot and dry. Linen hung damp creases far less, because gravity pulls the folds out while the hydrogen bonds are still mobile. The single most effective thing you can do to a linen shirt is take it out of the machine wet, shake it once, and hang it on a wide hanger.

Living with it

If you want a linen shirt to look pressed, press it. Warm iron, on the reverse, while the cloth is still slightly damp. It takes about ninety seconds and it will hold until you sit down. If that sounds like too much work for a garment you will crease again by lunchtime, that is a reasonable conclusion and it is the honest reason most people who love linen stop ironing it.

The alternative, which is what we do and what we design for, is to accept the crease as part of how the cloth reads. Our patterns are cut with that in mind. The Weaver Band-Collar Shirt has no collar stand, because a collar stand is the one part of a shirt that looks wrong when it is not pressed. Our trousers have a flat front and no permanent crease line, because a pressed crease in linen lasts about an hour and then looks like a mistake. The Long Linen Shift Dress has no waist seam, because a waist seam is where creasing concentrates when you sit.

None of that is styling. It is pattern cutting done with a realistic model of the fabric.

The part we got wrong for four years

Until 2023 our product pages said things like naturally relaxed texture and lived-in character. That is marketing language for a wrinkle, and dressing the thing up in a euphemism did two bad things. It set the expectation that the creasing would be gentle and decorative, which it is not, and it implied we were slightly embarrassed by it.

The pages now say the cloth creases, show it creased in the photographs, and explain what to do if you would rather it did not. Our return rate for creasing-related dissatisfaction went down, not up, which is the outcome you would expect once you stop over-promising. Our campaign photography is shot without pressing between frames for the same reason.

The crease is what a strong, cool, long-lived cellulose fibre does when you fold it. Suppressing it costs you strength, softening and lifespan. We would rather sell you cloth that outlasts the decade and tell you plainly how it will look at four in the afternoon.

Short version, if you skipped down

  • Linen creases because flax cellulose is highly crystalline and has almost no elastic recovery.
  • The strength that makes linen last is the same property that makes it crease.
  • Resin finishes stop the creasing and cost roughly 20 to 40 per cent of the fabric's strength.
  • Blending with viscose or polyester works and introduces pilling and wet-strength problems.
  • Heavier weights, twill and leno weaves, and hanging damp all reduce visible creasing.
  • Tumble drying is the single worst thing you can do to linen. Hang it wet instead.
